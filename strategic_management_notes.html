Evolution and Concept of Strategy

Strategy migrated from military thinking — the Greek strategos, the art of the general — into business in the mid-20th century. Early business strategy was largely long-range financial planning; it evolved through several distinct phases as the competitive environment grew more complex.

1950–60s

Budgeting & Financial Control

Annual budgets, variance analysis, cost control. Strategy as a finance exercise.

1960–70s

Corporate Planning

Long-range planning, portfolio matrices (BCG). Projecting trends; diversification focus.

1980s

Competitive Strategy

Porter's five forces and generic strategies. Industry structure as the primary determinant of performance.

1990–2000s

Resource-Based View

Barney, Wernerfelt — internal resources and capabilities as the source of sustained advantage.

2000s–now

Dynamic Capabilities & Ecosystems

Teece — ability to adapt and reconfigure in rapidly changing environments. Platform and network thinking.

Defining Strategy — Mintzberg's 5 Ps

Strategy resists a single definition. Henry Mintzberg's five perspectives capture the breadth of how the term is legitimately used:

P

Plan

Deliberate course of action decided in advance

P

Ploy

Specific manoeuvre to outwit a competitor

P

Pattern

Consistency in behavior over time, intended or not

P

Position

Locating the firm within its environment

P

Perspective

The firm's ingrained worldview and way of doing things

Working Definition

Strategy is the long-term direction of an organisation — making choices about where to compete, how to create value, and how to allocate resources to build sustainable competitive advantage.

The Strategic Management Process

The strategic management process is the full cycle by which strategy is formulated, implemented, and evaluated. It is iterative, not linear — evaluation feeds back into the next round of environmental analysis.

01

Environmental Analysis

External opportunities & threats; internal strengths & weaknesses

02

Strategy Formulation

Setting direction; choosing strategic options at corporate, business & functional levels

03

Implementation

Structure, systems, culture & resource allocation to translate strategy into action

04

Evaluation & Control

Measuring outcomes vs. objectives; feeding results back into the next cycle

Vision, Mission, and Strategic Intent

These three concepts form the directional foundation of any strategy, operating at different levels of specificity and time horizon.

Future-oriented

Vision

The aspirational, long-term picture of what the organisation wants to become. Inspirational and relatively stable. "To be the world's most loved, most trusted brand."

Current purpose

Mission

Defines what the organisation does, for whom, and why. More operational than vision, guiding day-to-day decisions. Specifies business scope, customer served, and value delivered.

Competitive stretch

Strategic Intent

Hamel & Prahalad (1989) — an ambitious competitive goal far beyond current capabilities, creating direction and energy. Explicitly competitive; demands creative tension. Example: Komatsu's intent to "Encircle Caterpillar."

Key Distinction

Vision asks "where do we want to go?" Strategic intent asks "what competitive position do we intend to seize, and what will it take to get there?" Strategic intent bridges today's reality to a future competitive position.

PESTLE Framework

PESTLE is a macro-environmental scanning tool that identifies external forces affecting an industry or organisation. It surfaces threats and opportunities but does not rank them — strategists must judge which factors are most significant for their specific context.

P — Political

Government stability, tax policy, trade regulations, foreign policy, labour law

E — Economic

GDP growth, interest rates, inflation, exchange rates, unemployment levels

S — Social

Demographic shifts, lifestyle changes, cultural attitudes, education, consumer values

T — Technological

R&D activity, automation, digital infrastructure, rate of technological change

L — Legal

Consumer protection, employment law, health & safety, data protection (GDPR)

E — Environmental

Carbon regulations, climate risk, resource scarcity, sustainability expectations

Porter's Five Forces

Michael Porter (1979) argued that industry profitability is determined by five structural forces. This is an industry-level tool — it explains why some industries are structurally more attractive than others. The collective strength of these forces determines how much profit can be earned by a typical competitor.

Force What it measures High when…
Competitive rivalry Intensity among existing firms Many balanced rivals, slow growth, high fixed costs, low differentiation, high exit barriers
Supplier power Bargaining leverage of suppliers Few suppliers, high switching costs, suppliers can integrate forward
Buyer power Bargaining leverage of customers Few buyers, large purchase volumes, undifferentiated products, substitutes available
Threat of new entrants Ease of entry into the industry Low economies of scale, low capital requirements, weak brand loyalty, no regulatory barriers
Threat of substitutes Alternative products or services Substitutes offer better price-performance, low switching costs for buyers
Strategic Implication

A strategy should position the firm to reduce the impact of these forces — or exploit weaknesses in competitors' positions relative to them. Porter's analysis is a starting point for identifying where to compete, not a complete strategy.

Resource-Based View & VRIO Framework

The RBV (Wernerfelt 1984; Barney 1991) shifts the unit of analysis inside the firm. Rather than asking "what industry should we be in?" it asks "what resources do we possess that can generate sustained competitive advantage?" Resources include physical assets, financial capital, human capital, brand equity, organisational culture, and intellectual property.

Barney's VRIO framework provides four tests a resource must pass to generate sustainable advantage:

RBV Implication

The RBV suggests competitive advantage is found by looking inward as much as outward. A firm should identify its VRIO resources and build strategy around protecting and leveraging them.

Teece's Dynamic Capabilities

The RBV was critiqued for being static — it explains current advantage but not how firms renew advantage in rapidly changing environments. David Teece (1997, 2007) responded with the Dynamic Capabilities framework.

Definition

Dynamic capabilities are the firm's ability to integrate, build, and reconfigure internal and external competences to address rapidly changing environments. They operate above ordinary capabilities to enable the firm to change what it does and how it does it.

Teece identifies three clusters:

Cluster 01

Sensing

Scanning, monitoring, and interpreting technological opportunities and competitive threats. Involves R&D, market research, and learning from customers and partners.
Cluster 02

Seizing

Mobilising resources to capture value from identified opportunities. Involves investment decisions, business model design, and commitment of capital and talent.
Cluster 03

Transforming

Ongoing renewal of assets, structures, and culture to remain aligned with a changing environment. Prevents rigidities from locking the firm into outmoded routines.

vs. RBV

Dynamic capabilities are about changing the resource base, not just deploying existing resources. A firm with strong dynamic capabilities continuously renews its sources of advantage.

Ordinary vs. Dynamic

Ordinary capabilities allow the firm to do routine things well. Dynamic capabilities operate at a higher level — enabling the firm to adapt those routines as circumstances change.

Outside-In

PESTLE and Porter's Five Forces look from the environment inward — identifying threats, opportunities, and structural attractiveness of industries.

Inside-Out

RBV and VRIO look from the firm outward — identifying which internal resources can be leveraged to create and sustain competitive advantage.

Dynamic capabilities bridge the two: they explain how firms adapt their internal resources in response to external shifts detected through PESTLE and Five Forces analysis. A firm that can sense environmental change, seize the resulting opportunity, and transform its capabilities accordingly sustains advantage even as the competitive landscape evolves.
